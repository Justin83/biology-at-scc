Cyanobacteria · Chapter 9

Cyanobacteria in Kentucky

▸ Outline · full text in Phase 2 Last reviewed 2026-11-05

Learning objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Summarize documented Kentucky HAB events and how advisories are issued.
  2. Describe Kentucky's freshwater settings: large reservoirs, the Ohio River, small lakes and farm ponds, karst streams.
  3. Distinguish documented Kentucky findings from hypotheses about Allen Branch or Lake Cumberland.
This chapter is an outline The introduction, planned sections, key terms and references below are reviewed and citable. Full textbook text for each section will be added in Phase 2.

Kentucky's waters include the Ohio River along its northern border, very large reservoirs built by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and the Tennessee Valley Authority, smaller lakes that supply drinking water, many farm ponds, and streams and springs in extensive karst landscapes. Each setting presents different cyanobacterial questions. This chapter separates what is documented about cyanobacteria in Kentucky from what is hypothesized — especially about the Somerset area, where documented information is scarce.

The documented record begins, in the Cabinet's public releases, in 2013, and includes large blooms on the Ohio River in 2015 and 2019 that led to recreational advisories along Kentucky's northern border [1, 2, 3]. Peer-reviewed Kentucky literature on cyanobacteria is thin — mostly veterinary case reports, one urban-pond study and one reservoir experiment [4, 5, 6, 7] — while nutrient studies in Kentucky's agricultural and karst basins are more extensive [8, 9, 10, 11].

What we did not find A search of peer-reviewed literature, USGS publications and the Cabinet's news releases (November 2026) found no study of cyanobacteria or cyanotoxins in Lake Cumberland or in Pulaski County streams. The nearest comparable work is downstream on the Cumberland River system in Tennessee, where USGS and state monitoring in a karst landscape detected at least one cyanotoxin at every one of 18 sites [17], and where microcosm experiments showed nitrogen and phosphorus co-limitation [18]. Absence of published studies is not evidence that blooms do not occur; it means the question is open.

Planned sections

9.1 Kentucky's freshwater environments

Large mainstem reservoirs (such as Kentucky Lake and Lake Cumberland), smaller water-supply lakes, the Ohio River, farm ponds, and karst streams and springs. Long-term nutrient and chlorophyll studies exist for Kentucky Lake [19, 20].

9.2 Historical HAB monitoring

From cell-count thresholds in 2013–2014 to toxin-based watch and warning levels by 2015–2016 (Chapter 5) [12, 21, 13, 15].

9.3 Major reservoirs and smaller ponds

Corps of Engineers lakes, municipal reservoirs and farm ponds; livestock cases from ponds [4, 5].

9.4 Agricultural watersheds

Statewide nutrient concentrations, loads and yields; agricultural versus forested basins [8, 11].

9.5 Karst and groundwater

Higher nutrient concentrations in intensely karst basins; slow response of a karst spring to best-management practices [8, 10].

9.6 Seasonal conditions

Summer low flow and warm temperatures on large rivers; seasonal nutrient peaks after fertilizer application [22, 9].

9.7 Public-health monitoring

Joint advisories by the Division of Water and Department for Public Health [3]; EPA guidance values; current thresholds to be confirmed with the Division of Water [23].

9.8 Relevant Kentucky and regional research

Ohio River risk modeling and USGS large-river sampling [22, 24]; Cumberland River studies in Tennessee [17, 18].

9.9 Remaining scientific questions

Lake Cumberland and its tributaries; benthic cyanobacteria in Kentucky streams; farm-pond surveys; links between karst nutrient delivery and cyanobacteria (Chapter 10).

Connection to local research

At Allen Branch and nearby waters

Hypothesis The Pitman Creek embayment of Lake Cumberland, where the USGS map routes the Allen Branch–Sinking Creek–Pitman Creek path, is the nearest place where planktonic cyanobacteria could plausibly bloom. Nothing is documented there in the sources reviewed for this chapter.

Allen Branch itself is not a documented bloom site. See the site profile and Chapter 10.

Key terms

Advisory
Public notice recommending people avoid certain water contact.
Watch / warning
Kentucky's two-tier advisory levels described in 2016 releases.
Karst
Landscape of dissolved limestone with sinkholes, springs and conduits.
Mainstem reservoir
A reservoir formed by damming a large river.
