Cyanobacteria · Chapter 6

Cyanobacteria and Aquatic Food Webs

▸ Outline · full text in Phase 2 Last reviewed 2026-11-05

Learning objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Place cyanobacteria in a freshwater food web alongside green algae and diatoms.
  2. Explain why cyanobacteria are often poor food: nutrition, shape, toxins and digestive inhibitors.
  3. Describe evidence for naturally tolerant Daphnia populations and the role of gut microbiota.
This chapter is an outline The introduction, planned sections, key terms and references below are reviewed and citable. Full textbook text for each section will be added in Phase 2.

In a healthy pond, green algae and diatoms are eaten by zooplankton such as rotifers, copepods and the water flea Daphnia, which are in turn eaten by fish; on stream rocks, snails and insect larvae graze the biofilm; and bacteria and fungi decompose what is not eaten. Cyanobacteria fit awkwardly into this web. Laboratory studies show that, as food, they generally support poorer zooplankton growth than small green algae and flagellates [1]. The reasons include a lack of key fatty acids and sterols, colonies and filaments too large or awkward to handle, toxins, and compounds that inhibit digestive enzymes [2, 3].

Yet blooms often coexist with abundant zooplankton in nature, and grazer responses are diverse and flexible [4]. Grazers adapt: Daphnia hatched from dated dormant eggs in Lake Constance showed that resistance to dietary cyanobacteria increased as the lake became eutrophic [5]; populations from lakes with toxic Microcystis tolerated it better than populations from lakes without [6]; and gut microbiota transplants showed that the microbiome can mediate tolerance [7].

Planned sections

6.1 Cyanobacteria, green algae and diatoms as food

Food quality differences among producers; edibility and size.

6.2 Rotifers, copepods and Daphnia

Different feeding modes and sensitivities; small-bodied grazers in bloom conditions [4, 8].

6.3 Aquatic snails and filter-feeding fish

Benthic grazers in streams; fish that filter plankton; effects on bloom dynamics.

6.4 Microbial decomposers

Bacterial breakdown of bloom biomass and the oxygen consequences (Chapter 5).

6.5 Why cyanobacteria are hard to eat

Nutritional quality, filaments and colonies, toxins, and digestive protease inhibitors that target trypsin and chymotrypsin in the Daphnia gut [9, 3, 1].

6.6 Naturally tolerant Daphnia

Rapid evolution, local adaptation and microbiome-associated tolerance [5, 6, 7].

6.7 Food webs on stream rocks

Grazing on biofilms and mats, and what is known about benthic cyanobacteria in stream food webs [10].

Connection to local research

At Allen Branch and nearby waters

Allen Branch is a stream, so its grazers are mostly benthic invertebrates rather than Daphnia; Daphnia work fits farm ponds and reservoir embayments better. Hypothesis Daphnia from ponds with frequent cyanobacteria will tolerate cyanobacterial food better than Daphnia from ponds without them — a contained laboratory comparison suited to undergraduates.

Related: native zooplankton, grazer interactions, Daphnia in the Atlas.

Key terms

Zooplankton
Small animals drifting in the water column.
Grazer
An animal that feeds on algae, cyanobacteria or biofilm.
Food quality
How well a food supports growth and reproduction.
Protease inhibitor
Compound that blocks digestive enzymes.
Local adaptation
Higher fitness of a population in its home environment.
