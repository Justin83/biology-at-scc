Cyanobacteria: Biology, Ecology, and Harmful Freshwater Blooms

For students in introductory biology, microbiology and environmental science Overview: about 15 minutes Last reviewed 2026-11-05

In late summer, a farm pond can turn the color of pea soup in a week. Downstream of a reservoir, a river can carry drifting green flecks for hundreds of kilometers. And on the rocks of a clear, cold stream, a dark leathery mat can be quietly producing a nerve toxin. All three are the work of cyanobacteria — an ancient group of photosynthetic bacteria that shaped Earth's atmosphere and still influence water quality, wildlife and human health today .

This unit is written as an open textbook. Its ten chapters can be read in order as a course, or used as a reference when a research question comes up in the field or the lab. Each chapter states its learning objectives, defines key terms, cites the sources behind its claims, ends with knowledge checks, and connects the science to research that students at Somerset Community College can do at Allen Branch and other south-central Kentucky waters.

The big ideas

1. Cyanobacteria are bacteria, not algae

Cyanobacteria photosynthesize the way algae and plants do, releasing oxygen, but their cells are prokaryotic: there is no nucleus, no chloroplast and no mitochondrion . "Blue-green algae" is a historical common name that persists in public advisories. Telling a cyanobacterium from a true alga is the first skill in this field, and the comparison table below summarizes it. Chapter 1 develops the cell biology.

2. They changed the planet — and live on in every chloroplast

Cyanobacteria invented oxygenic photosynthesis, and their oxygen accumulated in the atmosphere during the Great Oxidation Event roughly 2.4 billion years ago . Later, a cyanobacterium engulfed by an early eukaryote became the first chloroplast, the ancestor of the plastids in green algae and land plants .

3. A few traits explain where they win

Gas vesicles let planktonic species float toward light ; heterocytes let some fix atmospheric nitrogen ; akinetes let others wait out winter in sediment . Combined with tolerance of warm water and efficient light harvesting, these traits help cyanobacteria outcompete other phytoplankton in warm, calm, nutrient-rich waters . Chapter 2 shows how the same traits are used to identify genera.

4. Blooms have several causes acting together

Excess nutrients are the foundation of most freshwater blooms, but warmth, calm and stratified water, long water residence time and abundant light strongly modulate them . Which nutrient matters most — phosphorus, nitrogen or both — depends on the water body and season; many systems are co-limited . That is why this portal avoids single-cause explanations (Chapter 3, Chapter 4).

5. Not every bloom is toxic, and you cannot tell by looking

Toxin production is a property of particular strains, and toxic and non-toxic strains of the same species look identical under the microscope . Even the dominant genus of a bloom does not reliably predict which toxins are present . Toxins are measured, not inferred, and the U.S. EPA provides health-based guidance values for recreation and drinking water . Chapter 5 covers the toxins and how to interpret results.

6. Streams have their own cyanobacteria

Most public attention goes to scums on lakes, but cyanobacteria also grow as mats and films attached to the bottoms of streams and rivers, and these benthic mats can produce toxins too . A survey of 75 wadeable streams in the southeastern United States detected microcystins in 39% of them . For a shaded creek like Allen Branch, this is the most relevant kind of cyanobacterial ecology.

7. Grazers struggle with cyanobacteria — and can adapt

Cyanobacteria are generally poor food for zooplankton such as Daphnia, because of nutritional deficiencies, awkward colony and filament shapes, toxins and compounds that inhibit digestive enzymes . Yet grazer populations exposed to cyanobacteria can evolve tolerance within decades, and gut microbiomes can contribute . Chapter 6 explores these interactions.

8. Prevention starts in the watershed

Reducing nutrient inputs is the foundation of lasting bloom control; in-lake treatments such as mixing, algaecides or hydrogen peroxide can help in specific situations but treat symptoms, and many marketed products do little . Biological approaches, including grazer-based biomanipulation, are promising but context-dependent . Proposals involving genetically modified organisms remain experimental and would require careful ecological risk assessment before any release (Chapter 7).

9. Kentucky has a bloom history — and big gaps near Somerset

Kentucky's environmental and public-health agencies have issued harmful algal bloom advisories since at least 2013, when the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers identified a bloom on Taylorsville Lake , and large blooms on the Ohio River prompted advisories in 2015 and 2019 . Published research on cyanobacteria in the Lake Cumberland basin is scarce; the closest studies come from the Cumberland River system in Tennessee . That gap is an opportunity for SCC students (Chapter 9, Chapter 10).

Knowledge check

  1. A news story calls a lake bloom "toxic algae." Name two ways that phrase could be inaccurate.

    Show answer and explanation

    The organisms are probably cyanobacteria, which are bacteria rather than algae; and a bloom is not necessarily toxic — toxicity depends on the strains present and must be measured.

  2. Why does this portal avoid saying "phosphorus causes blooms"?

    Show answer and explanation

    Phosphorus is often important, but nitrogen, co-limitation by both nutrients, temperature, light, mixing and residence time also control blooms, and the balance differs among water bodies and seasons.

  3. Where in a shaded stream would you look first for cyanobacteria, and why?

    Show answer and explanation

    On submerged surfaces — rocks, wood, sediment — as attached biofilms or mats. Flowing water flushes plankton downstream, so benthic growth is more typical of streams than floating scums.
