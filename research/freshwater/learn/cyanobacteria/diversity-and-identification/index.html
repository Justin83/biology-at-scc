Cyanobacteria · Chapter 2

Cyanobacterial Diversity and Identification

✓ Full text About 25 minutes Last reviewed 2026-11-05

Cyanobacteria come in a remarkable range of shapes for organisms with no internal organelles: single spheres smaller than a red blood cell, jelly colonies of thousands of cells, straight or coiled chains, and tangled mats that can coat a riverbed. Their shapes and specialized cells are what we see under the microscope, so they are the starting point for identification. This chapter introduces the growth forms and structures, profiles eight genera students are likely to meet in Kentucky waters, and explains — just as importantly — what a microscope cannot tell you.

2.1Growth forms

Microscopists group cyanobacteria by how cells are arranged:

  • Unicellular — cells live singly or in pairs after division.
  • Colonial — many cells held together in a shared gelatinous mucilage. Microcystis colonies, for example, contain packed spherical cells in an irregular jelly mass that can be visible to the naked eye.
  • Filamentous — cells divide in one plane and stay attached end-to-end as a chain called a trichome. A trichome together with any surrounding sheath is a filament. Some filaments are straight, some coiled, some branched, and some can glide.

These categories are useful but not taxonomic in themselves: modern genetic studies show that similar shapes evolved repeatedly in unrelated lineages, which is one reason classifications keep changing .

2.2Specialized cells and structures

Four features do most of the work in identification, and each has an ecological meaning:

Heterocytes
Thick-walled, usually paler and evenly granular cells where nitrogen fixation happens. They lack oxygen-producing photosystem II, protecting the oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase enzyme. In the model strain Anabaena (Nostoc) PCC 7120, single heterocytes form at regular intervals of roughly 10 to 20 vegetative cells . Their position — along the filament (intercalary) or at its ends (terminal) — is diagnostic. Older texts call them heterocysts.
Akinetes
Enlarged, thick-walled resting cells packed with reserves, formed by members of the order Nostocales. Akinetes sink to the sediment, survive cold and drying, and can germinate to seed the next season's population . Their size, shape and position relative to heterocytes help separate genera and species.
Gas vesicles
Gas-filled protein cylinders that make cells buoyant; aggregates of them (sometimes called aerotopes) make cells look dark, irregular or shimmering in brightfield light. Gas vesicles let planktonic species regulate their depth — rising toward light and, in calm weather, accumulating as surface scums .
Mucilage and sheaths
Extracellular polysaccharide layers. A diffuse jelly holds colonies together (Microcystis); a firm, distinct sheath encloses filaments in many mat-forming genera; a tough common matrix gives Nostoc colonies their rubbery feel. India ink added to a wet mount makes mucilage visible as a clear halo.

Two patterns are worth remembering. First, the planktonic bloom-formers (Microcystis, Dolichospermum, Aphanizomenon, Planktothrix, Raphidiopsis) all have gas vesicles, which is how they stay in the light in still water . Second, the stream mat-formers (Microcoleus and Oscillatoria-like filaments) lack gas vesicles and heterocytes and grow attached to surfaces, which is why they matter more for a creek like Allen Branch .

Microcystis deserves special mention: it has been recorded blooming in at least 108 countries and is the most frequently cited producer of microcystins . Dolichospermum and Aphanizomenon, both nitrogen fixers, can gain an advantage when dissolved nitrogen is scarce relative to phosphorus — the pattern behind the classic observation that low nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratios favor cyanobacteria, and behind the rise of nitrogen-fixers when nitrogen inputs to an experimental lake were cut . Raphidiopsis (formerly Cylindrospermopsis) is notable for expanding from tropical into temperate waters .

2.4Why the names keep changing

Cyanobacteria were classified for over a century almost entirely by appearance. DNA sequencing revealed that many traditional genera mixed unrelated organisms, so names are being revised to match evolutionary relationships — a "polyphasic" approach that combines genes, morphology, ultrastructure and ecology . Between 2014 and 2021 alone, at least 273 new species in 140 genera were described . Three changes affect students in Kentucky directly:

  • Anabaena → Dolichospermum. Planktonic species with gas vesicles, formerly in Anabaena, were moved to the new genus Dolichospermum in 2009; Anabaena now refers mainly to benthic species without gas vesicles . Older reports — including Kentucky's 1989 livestock poisoning case, attributed to "Anabaena spiroides" — use the old name .
  • Cylindrospermopsis → Raphidiopsis. The two genera were separated only by whether heterocytes were present, a trait that can disappear. A 2018 polyphasic study supported uniting them, and C. raciborskii is now Raphidiopsis raciborskii .
  • Phormidium → Microcoleus. Molecular and morphological work moved the mat-forming Phormidium autumnale group into Microcoleus . River-mat literature from before about 2013 uses Phormidium.

Practical rule: when you read a paper, check the current name in AlgaeBase, and record both the name you used and the source you followed.

2.5What a microscope can and cannot tell you

Brightfield microscopy is the right first tool: it is inexpensive, fast and reveals growth form, cell dimensions, heterocytes, akinetes and gas vesicles. It also has firm limits that this portal treats as rules.

Limits of morphological identification
  • Species are often unresolvable. Colony shape, coiling and cell size vary with growth conditions and change in culture; many traditional morphospecies are not supported by genetic data . Identify to genus — or to a provisional morphotype — unless you have molecular evidence.
  • Toxicity is invisible. Microcystin-producing and non-producing genotypes of Microcystis coexist in the same lake and look the same; the proportion of toxic genotypes can differ between colony size classes . Toxin genes and toxin concentrations must be measured .
  • The dominant genus does not predict the toxins. In 23 Midwestern blooms, community composition was not strongly correlated with which toxins were present .
  • Look-alikes are common. Colonial green algae, filamentous green algae, diatoms in chains, and even floating plant debris can be mistaken for cyanobacteria in the field .

2.6A teaching key to growth forms

The steps below sort a wet-mount specimen into a growth form and a starting genus to check against the Atlas. It is a teaching tool, not a definitive key; consult the USGS field and laboratory guide for photographs of each group and its look-alikes .

  1. Are there distinct chloroplasts (sharply bounded green bodies), a visible nucleus or flagella? Yes → a eukaryotic alga or protist, not a cyanobacterium. No, pigment is diffuse → continue.
  2. Are the cells joined in chains (trichomes)? No → step 3. Yes → step 4.
  3. Single cells or a colony? Many small round cells with dark gas-vesicle clusters in an irregular jelly colony → check Microcystis. Without gas vesicles → other colonial coccoid genera; record as a morphotype.
  4. Are there heterocytes (pale, thick-walled cells)? Yes → step 5. No → step 6.
  5. Heterocytes along the chain: filaments in a firm jelly ball or sheet → Nostoc; free filaments with gas vesicles, round or barrel cells → Dolichospermum; straight filaments in bundles with elongated pale end cells → Aphanizomenon. Heterocytes only at the ends of short filaments → Raphidiopsis.
  6. No heterocytes: solitary straight filaments with gas vesicles, floating → Planktothrix (or Raphidiopsis lacking heterocytes); filaments without gas vesicles, attached or in mats, cells disc-shaped and gliding → Oscillatoria-like; several filaments bundled within one sheath, forming dark leathery stream mats → Microcoleus.

Record what you saw (form, dimensions in micrometers measured with a calibrated ocular micrometer, presence of each structure), your provisional identification, and your confidence. The Methods page explains calibration and photomicrography.

2.7Connection to local research

At Allen Branch

Hypothesis If cyanobacteria are present in Allen Branch, benthic, mat-forming filaments without heterocytes (Oscillatoria-like or Microcoleus-like) are more likely than planktonic bloom-formers, because the stream is flowing and shaded. This is a prediction to test, not a finding.

A good first dataset: scrape a measured area of rock at several stations, prepare wet mounts, photograph every distinct morphotype with a scale bar, and record provisional identifications for faculty review. See Chapter 10 and the observation records.

Key terms

Trichome
A chain of cells; with its sheath, a filament.
Mucilage
Extracellular jelly holding colonies or filaments together.
Heterocyte
Nitrogen-fixing cell; intercalary or terminal.
Akinete
Thick-walled resting cell of Nostocales.
Gas vesicle
Gas-filled protein structure providing buoyancy.
Morphotype
A group recognized by appearance; a provisional identification.
Polyphasic taxonomy
Classification combining genetic, morphological, ultrastructural and ecological data.

Knowledge check

  1. You see a straight filament of disc-shaped cells gliding slowly across the slide, with no heterocytes. What is the most defensible label, and why not a species name?

    Show answer and explanation

    "Oscillatoria-like filament" (a provisional morphotype). Several genera share this appearance, and species-level identification of such filaments needs more characters and usually molecular data.

  2. A pond sample is dominated by Microcystis colonies. A classmate concludes the pond is toxic. What is wrong with that reasoning?

    Show answer and explanation

    Toxic and non-toxic Microcystis strains look the same; toxin production depends on genotype and conditions. Only a toxin measurement (or, as supporting evidence, toxin-gene analysis) can show whether microcystins are present.

  3. Why do most planktonic bloom-formers have gas vesicles while stream mat-formers usually do not?

    Show answer and explanation

    In still water, buoyancy lets plankton stay in the lit surface layer; in a stream, cells attached to the bottom are held in place, and flowing water would carry floating cells away.

  4. A 2005 paper reports Anabaena flos-aquae blooms in a reservoir. What name would you check today, and where?

    Show answer and explanation

    Planktonic Anabaena species were transferred to Dolichospermum (e.g., D. flos-aquae). Check the current accepted name and synonyms in AlgaeBase.
