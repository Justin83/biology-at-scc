Monitoring & assessment Karst & groundwater Cyanobacterial blooms in Kentucky

Tracking Toxins: A Pilot Investigation of Cyanotoxins in North-Central Tennessee’s Surface Waters and Wells

Hill, K. L., Jaegge, A. C., Moore, D. M., & Byl, T. D. (2026). Toxins, 18(6), 239.

The closest published analog to the Somerset area: cyanotoxin monitoring in a karst landscape on the Cumberland River system downstream in Tennessee.

Study details
Geographic scope
North-central Tennessee, Cumberland basin (regional analog)
Organisms
Cyanobacteria
Methodology
USGS and Tennessee Dept. of Environment & Conservation sampled 18 sites, including two wells under surface-water influence, every two weeks (Sept 2022–Nov 2024).
Key findings
At least one cyanotoxin was detected at every site; the highest levels were in deep reservoirs, and dissolved microcystin was inversely related to Cumberland River water levels.
Limitations
Tennessee sites — an analog for, not evidence about, Lake Cumberland or Pulaski County waters.

Nutrient concentration, stoichiometry, and timing of delivery can regulate cyanobacterial dominance and microcystin production in rivers

Li, J., & Murdock, J. (2025). Journal of Environmental Management, 377, 124714.

Experimental evidence from the Cumberland River that the amount, ratio and timing of N and P shape which cyanobacteria win.

Study details
Geographic scope
Cumberland River, Tennessee (regional analog)
Organisms
Microcystis; Dolichospermum
Methodology
36-day incubations of Cumberland River water across four nitrogen–phosphorus gradient scenarios.
Key findings
Phytoplankton were co-limited by N and P; N-fixing Dolichospermum dominated at very low N:P, while Microcystis dominated when both nutrients were added.
Limitations
Laboratory incubations of one river's water.

Cyanobacterial blooms in Kentucky Nutrients & agricultural watersheds

Cyanobacteria Growth in Nitrogen- & Phosphorus-Spiked Water from a Hypereutrophic Reservoir in Kentucky, USA

Hughes, S. E., & Marion, J. W. (2021). Journal of Environmental Protection, 12(02), 75-89.

A Kentucky reservoir experiment illustrating that the limiting nutrient for cyanobacteria can be nitrogen rather than phosphorus at a given time and place.

Study details
Geographic scope
Kentucky (Guist Creek Lake)
Organisms
Cyanobacteria (phycocyanin)
Methodology
Laboratory microcosms of reservoir water spiked with nitrogen and/or phosphorus; cyanobacteria tracked as phycocyanin.
Key findings
Phosphate alone did not increase cyanobacteria, while added nitrogen did, suggesting phosphorus was not limiting when the water was collected.
Limitations
Short microcosm experiment from one reservoir and date; the authors call for more robust designs. Published by SCIRP — weigh the venue.

Cyanobacterial blooms in Kentucky Microcystis ecology Monitoring & assessment

Harmful Algae Bloom Occurrence in Urban Ponds: Relationship of Toxin Levels with Cell Density and Species Composition

de la Cruz, A., Logsdon, R., Lye, D., Guglielmi, S., Rice, A., & Kannan, M. S. (2017). Journal of Earth and Environmental Science, 25, 704-726.

Kentucky evidence that small constructed ponds can host Microcystis blooms year-round and that cell counts are a poor stand-in for toxin concentration.

Study details
Geographic scope
Northern Kentucky (urban ponds)
Organisms
Microcystis
Methodology
Year-long monitoring (Oct 2012–Sep 2013) of algal communities, basic water quality and microcystin in several urban retention ponds.
Key findings
Blooms were mostly Microcystis and occurred throughout the year; basic water-quality variables (including nitrate and soluble reactive phosphate) did not correlate with cyanobacteria or microcystin, and toxin levels did not always track Microcystis cell counts.
Limitations
Small number of ponds in one urban area; the DOI listed on the PubMed record does not resolve, so cite by PMID/PMCID.
